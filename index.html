Breaking the Chain

Distress tolerance skills and the step-by-step path to behaviors you want to change

Welcome

8 to 10 minAbout today

Every behavior has a chain of events leading up to it. A vulnerability, a trigger, a cascade of thoughts and feelings, and then the behavior itself. Today we learn two things: how to map that chain so you can see it clearly, and where to insert distress tolerance skills so the chain breaks before it reaches the behavior you're trying to change.

These are crisis survival tools, not permanent solutions. They buy you time.

Chain analysis

15 to 20 minHow behaviors happen

A behavioral chain has five parts. Every binge, every purge, every episode of restriction follows this sequence. Once you can see the chain, you can find the links where you have leverage.

1
Vulnerability factors
What made you more susceptible that day? Didn't sleep, skipped breakfast, fought with a parent, hadn't talked to anyone in hours. These are the conditions, not the cause.
2
Prompting event
The specific thing that set the chain in motion. A comment, a feeling, something you saw, a situation you walked into. One identifiable moment.
3
Links: thoughts, feelings, body sensations
"I'm disgusting" (thought) → chest tightness (body) → shame (feeling) → "I need this to stop" (thought) → numbness creeping in (body). Each link connects to the next.
4
The behavior
The binge, the purge, the restriction, the exercise. This is where the chain ends up if nothing interrupts it.
5
Consequences
What happened after. Temporary relief (the reinforcer), then guilt, physical discomfort, shame, avoidance. The consequences often become vulnerability factors for the next chain.
Clinical note

The behavior itself was functioning as a distress tolerance skill. It was the way you managed an unbearable feeling. Chain analysis doesn't judge that. It reveals the function so you can find alternatives that don't cost as much.

Distress tolerance

10 to 15 minTools for the hardest moments

These skills are not designed to fix the problem or make you feel better. They keep you from making it worse. That distinction matters. When the urge is at its peak, you don't need insight. You need 90 seconds of survival.

T
Temperature
Cold resets your nervous system

Hold ice cubes. Splash cold water on your face. Press a cold pack to your neck. The cold triggers your dive reflex, which slows your heart rate and pulls blood away from your extremities toward your core. Your body shifts from fight-or-flight to settle. This works in under 30 seconds.

For eating disorder recovery: use before meals when anxiety peaks, not as a replacement for eating. The goal is to lower the alarm so you can sit down.
I
Intense exercise
Burn off the activation

Sprint, do jumping jacks, run stairs, push against a wall as hard as you can. When your body is flooded with adrenaline and cortisol, intense physical activity uses those chemicals for what they were designed for: movement. Five to ten minutes is enough.

For eating disorder recovery: this is crisis exercise, not compensatory exercise. The purpose is nervous system regulation, not calorie burn. If exercise is part of your ED pattern, talk to your team about alternatives.
P
Paced breathing
Long exhale, short inhale

Breathe in for 4 counts. Breathe out for 6 to 8 counts. The exhale is the key. Every exhale activates your vagus nerve, which tells your body the threat is over. Five slow breaths can shift your nervous system state in under a minute.

Practice when you're NOT in crisis so it becomes automatic. Your body needs to recognize the pattern before it can use it under pressure.
P
Paired muscle relaxation
Tense and release

Tense one muscle group (fists, shoulders, legs) while breathing in. Release everything on the exhale. The contrast between tension and release teaches your body what "settled" feels like. Work through your whole body in two to three minutes.

Pairs well with paced breathing. The physical release gives your body a concrete sensation of letting go.

Group process

10 minGoing deeper

Patterns

When you think about your own chains, where do they usually start? Is it a vulnerability factor, a specific trigger, or do you only notice once you're already deep in the links?

Most people notice late. That's normal. The goal is to move awareness earlier over time, not to catch every chain at the first link.

Skills

Which TIPP skill sounds most doable for you right now? Which one sounds impossible?

The impossible one is worth exploring. Sometimes resistance points to the skill they need most.

Function

If your eating disorder behavior is doing the job of a distress tolerance skill, what feeling is it managing for you?

This is the core question. If they can name the feeling the behavior manages, they can find a TIPP skill that targets the same feeling.

Barriers

What gets in the way of using a skill when you actually need one?

Common answers: I forget, I don't believe it will work, the urge is too strong, I don't care in the moment. Each barrier has a different solution.

This week

5 to 8 minPick one

Choose one experiment. You're not mastering a skill. You're trying it once, in one moment, and seeing what happens.

Try TIPP once
Next time an urge hits, use one TIPP skill before acting on it. Ice, sprinting, breathing, tensing. Just one.
Map one chain
After a difficult moment this week, write down the chain: vulnerability, trigger, links, behavior, consequences. No judgment. Just the map.
Spot the vulnerability
Each morning, check in: am I tired, hungry, lonely, or stressed? Those conditions make chains more likely. Name them before they run.
Surf one urge
When an urge peaks, set a timer for 90 seconds. Breathe through it. See if the wave comes down even slightly. It usually does.
